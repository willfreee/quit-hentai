Chapter 7: It's Just Pixels

You're right. It is just pixels.

No one is being filmed without consent. No one is being trafficked, exploited, or coerced. No real bodies are being bought, sold, or harmed in the making of what you consume. The ethical concerns that surround real pornography—and they are serious, and they are real—don't apply here. If the question is "who is being hurt by the production of this content," the answer is genuinely, factually: no one.

That's a real distinction. It matters. You're not wrong to recognize it.

But it's not the question that matters.

The question that matters is: what is it doing to you?

Water is ethically neutral. No one is harmed by the production of water. It doesn't exploit workers. It doesn't traffic vulnerable people. It's just water. Drink too much of it—far too much, in a short span—and it still kills you. The safety of the substance's source tells you absolutely nothing about the safety of consuming it in excess. The ethics of production and the effects of consumption are two completely different things.

And you have been using the first to avoid looking at the second.

This is the chapter where we stop talking about whether the pixels are ethical—they are, or at least they can be—and start talking about whether they're harmless. Not harmless in principle. Not harmless in theory. Harmless to you. To your brain. To the person you're becoming.

Because the screen is not the substance. The screen is the delivery system. And what's being delivered is rewiring your mind whether the images are photographs or drawings.

Let me grant the ethical premise fully before I say anything else.

Hentai occupies an unusual position in the landscape of compulsive sexual consumption. The people in real porn are real. Their bodies are real. What happens to them happens to someone. The ethical weight of that is significant, and many consumers of real porn carry a quiet guilt about it—a sense that their consumption is, however indirectly, connected to someone's suffering.

You don't carry that particular weight. Or at least, you've been able to tell yourself you don't.

And in one sense, that's legitimate. A drawing doesn't have a life that can be ruined. A fictional scenario doesn't leave trauma in its wake. An artist working at a tablet isn't being coerced in the way an actor on a set might be. There are real ethical distinctions here, and I'm not going to pretend they don't exist or tell you they don't matter.

The problem is not that the ethical argument is false. The problem is that you've been using it to answer a question it has nothing to do with.

"Who is harmed by the production of this content?" That's a question about other people.

"What is this content doing to me?" That's a question about you.

And those are not the same question. They're not even in the same category. The first is about ethics. The second is about neuroscience. You can be ethically flawless and still be destroying yourself. The two domains don't cancel each other out. They don't even interact.

You've been pointing to the clean ethics of the production as if that resolves the question of the consumption. It doesn't. It never did. It was just the most elegant way the Big Monster found to make you stop asking the question that actually matters.

Let's talk about the screen.

When you sit down and open your device and start browsing, what's actually happening? Not in terms of morality or meaning—in terms of mechanism. What is the interaction between you and the pixels?

The pixels are a delivery system. They are a way of getting something into your brain. That something is not ink or light or digital information. That something is a pattern of neural activation. The screen is not the substance. The screen is the needle.

Of course not. The needle is just how the substance gets in. The substance is what does the damage. The ethics of the delivery system are irrelevant to the effects of what's being delivered.

You've spent a lot of time thinking about the needle. Whether it's clean. Whether it's ethical. Whether using drawings instead of photographs makes you a more responsible consumer. And the whole time, the substance was doing exactly what the substance does—regardless of the needle's moral status.

The dopamine system doesn't know what pixels are. It doesn't know what a drawing is. It doesn't distinguish between a photograph of a real person and an artistic rendering of a fictional one. The needle is clean. The substance is still the substance.

Your brain doesn't care about the difference.

If it looks like a sexual opportunity, if it feels novel and exciting, if it triggers the ancient machinery of arousal and pursuit, the dopamine flows. Whether the image is a photograph or a drawing is a distinction your neocortex makes. Your reward system doesn't make it. It can't.

The pixels aren't protecting your brain. Your brain doesn't know what a pixel is. Your brain knows novelty, intensity, and anticipation. And the pixels deliver all three.
